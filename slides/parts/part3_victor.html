Block 5 · Audit · why not just ask the judge?

In one study, a judge's hidden states predicted its errors better than its words

Better calibrated

Brier-trained linear probes beat verbalized and multi-sample confidence, at about 10× less compute.

Transfers — there

On their reasoning judges, it held up on unseen domains. You will test that on ours.

Conservative

Under-confident on easy sets — a fair price when a missed error costs more than a review.

LLM judges are overconfident in words. The cheap baseline every probe must beat: the judge's own Yes/No token probability.

Brier score = mean squared gap between confidence and the 0/1 outcome (lower is better). Radharapu et al., “Calibrating LLM Judges: Linear Probes for Fast and Reliable Uncertainty Estimation”, ACL 2026 Industry, arXiv 2512.22245. Related: semantic entropy probes, Kossen, Han et al. 2024, arXiv 2406.15927.

2:16:45 → 2:17:30. The R-PM-07 story is invented; this result is not. Radharapu et al. train linear probes on reasoning judges' hidden states with a Brier-score loss. Compared with verbalized confidence and multi-generation methods: better calibration, ≈10× cheaper, and in their experiments it generalized to unseen evaluation domains. Do not sell transfer as a general fact: in our own test run (NB5 fallback judge) the probe did NOT transfer to held-out assertions — foreshadow: "you will test whether it transfers". Their honest caveat: probes are conservative and underperform on easier datasets — which suits safety-critical settings that prioritise low false positives. Audits are such a setting. Token probability: for a Yes/No judge, P(Yes) from the output logits is free and often well-ranked; in NB5 it is the baseline to beat, alongside verbalized confidence. A probe is only interesting if it carries information beyond the output probability (higher AUROC, since recalibration can fix ECE but not ranking). Semantic entropy probes (Kossen, Han et al.) do the same trick for hallucination: predict uncertainty from one forward pass instead of many samples.

Synthetic illustration · not measured

A calibrated score turns review into a dial

2:17:30 → 2:19:30. Centrepiece. Synthetic data (say so): 3,000 simulated verdicts, judge accuracy ≈ 74%. Nothing on this slide is measured. Left = reliability diagram: x = stated confidence, y = how often the verdict is actually correct; the diagonal is perfect calibration. Step 0: verbalized confidence piles up at 0.8–1.0 but accuracy stays near 0.75 → bars far below the diagonal = overconfident. → Step 1: the probe's bars hug the diagonal. → Step 2: right = risk–coverage. Auto-accept every verdict whose probe score ≥ τ. Coverage = share accepted automatically; y = error rate among accepted. Threshold low: accept nearly everything, every error escapes. → Step 3 and 4: raise τ. Fewer automatic acceptances, far fewer escaped errors. Read the line under the chart aloud: 'escaped errors' = verdicts that are wrong AND auto-accepted, as a share of all verdicts (NB5's definition, and the escape rate of the next slide). The dashed line is NB5's routing target: at most 10% error among auto-accepted verdicts. In the synthetic data the probe curve meets the 10% target at about 38–40% coverage (step 4, τ = 0.83: auto-accept 38%, error among accepted ≈ 9.9%, escaped errors 3.8% of all verdicts). Routing by verbalized confidence only gets under 10% at tiny coverage (below ~8%), because its scores barely separate right from wrong. Step 3 (τ = 0.62): 74% coverage but ≈ 18% error among accepted — above target. Point at the grey curve: "Same coverage, routing by verbalized confidence lets more errors through." Block 5 · Audit · from score to guarantee

Research direction · not a published result

A conformal threshold could bound how many wrong verdicts escape review

Lowest τ with (n/(n+1))·escape(τ) + 1/(n+1) ≤ α on calibration data → expected escape rate ≤ α.

Holds only if new cases look like calibration cases. A new process breaks that: test it.

2:19:30 → 2:20:15. Say clearly: "This is a research direction — my thesis line, LLM-assisted quality and security audits of software projects — not a published result." → Step 1: a calibration set with gold labels: n = 60 synthetic verdicts, placed by probe score. Grey = the judge was right, red = wrong. Most red dots sit at low scores, but not all. → Step 2: an escape is a verdict that is wrong AND auto-accepted (score ≥ τ); the escape rate is escapes over ALL verdicts (NB5's definition, same as the A10 readout). Rule, exactly NB5's crc_threshold: scan thresholds from low to high and take the first (lowest) τ with (n/(n+1))·escape_cal(τ) + 1/(n+1) ≤ α. The 1/(n+1) term is the finite-sample price; with n = 60 it already uses 1.6 of the 10 points, so the empirical escape budget is about 8.5%. Read the line under the axis: how many red dots are ringed (escape), the bound, and how many verdicts are auto-accepted. This is conformal risk control with the probe score: if new verdicts are exchangeable with calibration verdicts, the EXPECTED escape rate on new verdicts is ≤ α (in expectation over calibration sets, not for every audit). Same recipe as the optional stretch at the end of NB5. Note for experts: this bounds escapes over all verdicts, not the share of errors that escape (a miss rate). With a ~74%-accurate judge, α = 10% of all verdicts can still be a large share of the errors; choose α with the auditor. → Step 3: the guarantee needs exchangeability. A new 29110 process, or a held-out assertion in NB5, is exactly where it can fail. That is the distribution-shift test you will run.
