Primer · 1 / 5 · tokens and embeddings

Text becomes tokens, tokens become vectors

0:08–0:08:45 (45 s). Plain words: a model never sees letters, only numbers.
Step 1: the tokenizer cuts the text into pieces from a fixed list of 50,257. Common words are one token; rare words are split: “Eiffel” is three tokens (·E, iff, el). The dot means “starts with a space”. Each piece has an id number.
Step 2: the id is just a row number into a big table, the embedding matrix (50,257 rows × 768 columns). Common tokens have small ids, so they all sit near the top.
Step 3: that row is the token's vector: 768 numbers. We show the first 8, real GPT-2 values; we draw vectors standing up as columns. GPT-2 also adds a position vector (another 768 numbers) so the model knows word order. From here on, “a token” inside the model means “a column of 768 numbers”.
If asked: TransformerLens also prepends an <|endoftext|> token at position 0; we leave it out for space. Values from scratch/primer/primer_extra.py (raw GPT-2 weights; TransformerLens's default loading centres them, which changes the third decimal).

0:08:45–0:09:45 (1 min). Everything on this slide is illustrative: 2 of 768 dimensions, made-up numbers, and “French-ness” is a teaching label, not a measured feature.
Step 0: an activation is a list of numbers; geometrically it is an arrow. Step 1: pick a direction d (an arrow of length 1). Step 2: the dot product x · d is the length of x's shadow on d: “how much of d is in x”. This is the one operation to remember. Right panel: the logit lens dots x with each token's unembedding vector; a probe dots x with a learned weight vector (plus a bias); an SAE feature's activation is x dotted with its encoder direction, plus a bias, then a (Jump)ReLU. Steering is the reverse: it writes along a direction (adds α·d to x); Salvador shows it in Block 3, where d is a feature's decoder direction.
Step 3: a different prompt gives a different arrow; the shadow shrinks and here goes slightly negative (−0.4).
Honest caveat for later: a direction that reads well is not proof the model uses it (Block 2, Block 3).

Primer · 3 / 5 · the output

Logits are scores; softmax turns them into probabilities

logittok = x · utok

x: final vector at “·of”. utok: one vector per vocabulary token.

logittok = x · utok

50,257 scores, any real number (25,379 negative).

ptok = exp(logittok)
      / Σt exp(logitt)

Softmax: all positive, sum to 1.

LD = logitParis − logitLyon
= 18.06 − 17.29 = 0.77

Linear in x, unlike probabilities. Block 2 measures with LD.

Real values: GPT-2 small, TransformerLens (logits centred to mean 0 over the vocabulary), scratch/primer/primer_numbers.py.

0:09:45–0:11 (1 min 15 s). Real numbers, GPT-2 small, prompt “The Eiffel Tower is in the French city of”.
Step 0: at the last position the model has one final vector x (after the last LayerNorm). It compares x with every word in its vocabulary by a dot product: one score per token, called a logit. (In GPT-2 the unembedding vectors are the embedding rows again: tied weights.)
Step 1: logits are any real number; about half of the 50,257 are negative (“·banana”: −1.34). Top: ·Paris 18.06, ·Nice 17.68, ·Lyon 17.29, ·Marse (first piece of “Marseille”) 16.80, ·L 15.91, ·Saint 15.80; ·London is only rank 176.
Step 2: softmax = exponentiate each logit, divide by the total. Now they are probabilities: Paris 0.207, Nice 0.141, Lyon 0.096 … and the other 50,249 tokens together still hold 0.45. Exponentiating means only differences between logits matter: Hugging Face reports these same logits shifted by −116.4 (TransformerLens centres them), and the probabilities are identical.
Step 3: so we compare two candidates directly: the logit difference, here 0.77 = ln(0.207/0.096). It is a linear function of x: add a vector v to x and LD moves by exactly v · (uParis − uLyon). A probability depends on all 50,257 logits at once, so it is not. That is why Salvador's patching metric in Block 2 is a logit difference (Mary vs John). Fine print if asked: x is taken after the final LayerNorm, whose rescaling is the one non-linear step between the residual stream and the logits.

Primer · 4 / 5 · vocabulary for the day

A transformer is layers that add to a running sum

Residual stream
the running sum: 768 numbers per position
Attention
moves information between positions (12 heads)
MLP
transforms one position at a time
Layer
one attention + one MLP; GPT-2 small stacks 12
Activation
the numbers at one layer and one position
0:11–0:11:30 (30 s). This is the map for the whole day; the next slide zooms in. Read the vocabulary, one word per click.
Step 0: embed at the bottom, unembed at the top, and the residual stream in between: one vector per position that every part reads from and adds to. Step 1: attention is the only part that moves information between positions; each layer has 12 heads working in parallel (Block 2 finds specific heads such as L9H9 = layer 9, head 9). Step 2: the MLP works on each position separately. Step 3: attention + MLP = one layer; GPT-2 small stacks 12 (counted 0–11). Step 4: an activation is the vector of numbers at one layer and one position, e.g. the stream after layer 6 at “·of”. (Attention and MLP outputs are activations too; today it usually means the stream.) All of today's methods take an activation and do something to it.

Block 1 · Observe

Every layer reads from one shared stream and adds to it

0:12–0:14:30 (2 min 30 s). The primer already named the parts; here, show them working. One vertical line per token position = the residual stream at that position (GPT-2 small: a 768-dim vector). Note the real tokenisation: “Eiffel” is three tokens.
Step 1: embeddings are written onto the streams. Step 2: attention is the only component that moves information between positions — here “of” reads from “Tower”, “French”, “city” and adds the result to its own stream. Step 3: the MLP works on one position at a time; it reads the stream and adds a vector back (MLPs are where many stored associations seem to live). Step 4: this repeats for 12 layers; at the end, unembedding reads the final stream at the last position and gives next-token probabilities.
Key sentence: the residual stream is a running sum — nothing overwrites it, every component adds to it. That is what makes the logit lens possible in 3 minutes. Panel on the right = the stream at “of” growing as a sum (segment sizes are schematic).
Accuracy note if asked: in real fact recall (Meng et al. 2022; Geva et al. 2023) early-middle MLPs enrich the subject token (“Tower”) and attention later copies the attribute to the last token; the figure compresses that story to one attention and one MLP step.

Block 1 · Observe · logit lens

Decoding every layer shows “Paris” emerging late

0:15:30–0:17:30 (2 min). Method in one sentence: take the residual stream at the last position after block ℓ, apply the model's own final LayerNorm and unembedding, and read it as if the model stopped there (nostalgebraist, 2020). It works because the stream is a running sum written in (roughly) the same basis the output reads.
These are real numbers, GPT-2 small, prompt “The Eiffel Tower is in the French city of”, computed with TransformerLens (scratch/p1/logit_lens.py). Step 1: early layers predict generic “ the”; P(Paris)≈0. Step 2: blocks 4–7 drift toward place-like tokens (“ La”, “ Man”, “ Rome”). Step 3: block 8 jumps to “ Paris” (0.75), peaks at block 9 (0.96), then the last blocks lower it to 0.21 at the output while keeping it top-1 (late layers spread mass to other cities — “ Nice”, “ Lyon” — a known calibration-like effect). Honest limits

Observation shows correlation, not cause

The logit lens can tell us

By block 8, the stream already points toward “ Paris”.

It cannot tell us

That block 8 causes the answer, or which component computes it.

  • Attention patterns show where a head looks, not what it moves or whether it matters.
  • Early layers decode poorly: their basis drifts from the output's. The tuned lens learns a translator per layer.

nostalgebraist (2020) “interpreting GPT: the logit lens”. Belrose et al. (2023) “Eliciting Latent Predictions from Transformers with the Tuned Lens”. Jain & Wallace (2019) “Attention is not Explanation”.

0:28–0:29 (1 min). Everything in Block 1 was reading. A thermometer that shows the temperature rising does not show what heats the room. Two technical caveats: (1) attention weights ≠ importance — a head can look at a token and write nothing useful; (2) the logit lens is biased toward late layers because early representations are not in the unembedding's basis; the tuned lens (Belrose et al.) fixes that with a learned affine map per layer. To claim cause we must intervene — change the activation and watch the output. That is Salvador's block.
